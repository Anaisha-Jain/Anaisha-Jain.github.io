Kinetik: Your Very Own Supplement Tracker

Overview

Kinetik is a mobile app I designed and built with Flutter and Firebase. Instead of only logging what you took, it models how supplements and caffeine behave in your body and graphs their concentration over time, personalised to your age, weight and metabolism.

It brings together mobile development, backend and database design, scientific modelling and data visualization in one project.

The problem

Most health and habit trackers are static. They record what you consumed and when, but not what that amount is doing inside your body. Two people who take the same dose can have very different amounts in their blood at 4 pm, and a log can't show that.

The field that answers this is pharmacokinetics: how a substance is absorbed, spread through the body and cleared over time. After a dose, the concentration rises, peaks and then decays, and the speed of each stage depends on the substance and the person. I wanted to put that model in a user's pocket, in a form anyone could read.

What I built

Kinetik takes a user's age, weight and metabolism, plus a log of what they take, and turns it into a live graph of how much of each substance is in their system. That graph powers the rest of the app.

Live concentration curves

Users log supplements, caffeine and similar substances, and the app plots a smooth concentration curve for each one. The curve shows when absorption is at its best, so a user can see at a glance when a dose is actually working in their body.

Limits and safety

The app tells users when to stop consuming for the day, warns about stacking substances that are processed by the same liver enzymes (CYP450), and helps time intake around sleep.

Focus tools

A focus tab helps users get the most out of their working hours, based on the career path they pick. It includes a Pomodoro-style timer built into the app (the Focus Bio-Timer).

Insights and engagement

A trends view shows long-term patterns as a contribution-style heatmap, an AI coach chat answers questions about the user's metabolic data, and a gamified lab-gear shop keeps people coming back.

How it works

The model

Concentration over time is computed with the Bateman equation, the standard one-compartment model for a substance taken by mouth. It describes the rise and fall of a dose as two competing processes: absorption into the blood and elimination out of it.

C(t) = [ F · D · kₐ / ( V · (kₐ − kₑ) ) ] · ( e^(−kₑt) − e^(−kₐt) )

  • C(t): concentration at time t after the dose
  • D, F: the dose, and the fraction of it that reaches the bloodstream
  • kₐ: the absorption rate
  • kₑ: the elimination rate, which comes from the substance's half-life (kₑ = ln 2 / half-life)
  • V: the volume the substance spreads through, which is why body weight matters

The app evaluates these modelling equations locally to simulate half-life, absorption rates and clearance. This turns plain timestamps in the intake log into smooth, dynamic line charts.

The architecture

The front end is written in Flutter and Dart, which gives one codebase for iOS and Android. User profiles, multi-substance intake logs and granular concentration data points live in a NoSQL schema I designed in Firebase Firestore, with Cloud Functions handling logic on the server.

The hardest part

The biggest technical challenge was translating abstract pharmacokinetic formulas into a real-time graph on a phone while keeping Firestore reads and writes optimized. It meant treating the app as both a data-modelling problem and a mobile-engineering problem: the maths has to be right, the graph has to stay smooth, and the database has to stay efficient.

Outcome

The finished app takes a person's age, weight and metabolism and graphs curves that show when each supplement is being absorbed and used best. It tells them when to cap their consumption and gives them a focus tab to plan their work around those curves.

The project taught me how to connect complex data modelling to clean mobile development, and how to make scientific maths understandable on a small screen.
